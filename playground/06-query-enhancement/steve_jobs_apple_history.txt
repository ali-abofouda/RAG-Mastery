# The History of Steve Jobs at Apple Computer

## Early Career and the Creation of Apple
In 1976, Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak co-founded Apple Computer in the Jobs family garage in Los Altos, California.
Their early products, the Apple I and Apple II, became foundational microcomputers that propelled the personal computer revolution.
Jobs was widely recognized as the visionary product strategist, driving intuitive user interfaces, industrial design, and packaging.
However, early management felt Jobs was temperamental and lacked the administrative discipline required to manage a rapidly scaling corporate enterprise.

## The Macintosh Division and the Hiring of John Sculley
By the early 1980s, Apple was preparing the Macintosh computer, which featured a revolutionary graphical user interface (GUI) and mouse.
In 1983, Steve Jobs personally recruited John Sculley, the former president of Pepsi-Cola, to serve as Apple's Chief Executive Officer.
Jobs famously convinced Sculley to join by asking: "Do you want to sell sugar water for the rest of your life, or do you want to come with me and change the world?"
Initially, Jobs and Sculley maintained a productive and close working relationship, sharing an ambitious vision for Apple's future.
Jobs led the Macintosh division as general manager, cultivating a passionate and intense pirate-flag culture within the team.

## Internal Power Struggle and Ouster in 1985
Despite widespread acclaim for its 1984 commercial and graphical innovation, the initial Macintosh suffered from sluggish commercial sales.
The computer was expensive, lacked sufficient internal RAM, had no hard drive, and offered limited third-party software support.
Tensions flared between Jobs and the rest of Apple's executive leadership, particularly with the Apple II division, which was generating almost all company revenue.
John Sculley and the board of directors, led by Arthur Rock and Mike Markkula, began to view Jobs as erratic, demanding, and disruptive to operations.
In May 1985, Steve Jobs attempted a boardroom coup while Sculley was scheduled to travel to China, seeking to replace Sculley as CEO.
However, Sculley discovered the plot, canceled his trip, and confronted Jobs directly during a contentious board meeting.
The board of directors unanimously sided with Sculley, officially stripping Steve Jobs of all managerial and operational duties over the Macintosh division.
Jobs was relegated to an honorary title as chairman, confined to an isolated office nicknamed "Siberia."
In September 1985, feeling completely marginalized and powerless, Steve Jobs officially resigned from Apple, sold all but one of his shares, and founded NeXT Computer.

## The NeXT Era and the Triumphant Return in 1997
During his decade away from Apple, Jobs built NeXT, which developed the advanced object-oriented NeXTSTEP operating system.
He also funded and acquired Pixar Animation Studios, which revolutionized digital cinema with the release of Toy Story in 1995.
Meanwhile, Apple struggled through the 1990s with declining market share, bureaucratic stagnation, and failed operating system modernizations.
In December 1996, under CEO Gil Amelio, Apple announced the acquisition of NeXT for 429 million dollars to acquire NeXTSTEP as the foundation for Mac OS X.
Steve Jobs returned to Apple initially as an unpaid informal advisor, but by mid-1997, the board ousted Gil Amelio and appointed Jobs as interim CEO (iCEO).
Under Jobs' second tenure, Apple simplified its product grid, partnered with Microsoft, launched the iMac, iPod, iPhone, and iPad, transforming Apple into the most valuable technology company in the world.
